      II. THIRD-PARTY OBJECTION CANNOT JUSTIFY DENIAL
      
Section 11
 of the RTI Act is purely procedural and can be invoked only after
      the CPIO records a prima facie satisfaction that the information sought
      attracts any exemption under 
Section 8(1)
, particularly 
Section 8(1)(j)
. It is
      mandatory for the CPIO to first examine whether the information
      constitutes "personal information"

      in light of the settled judicial interpretation of 
Section 8(1)(j)
, and only upon
      an affirmative finding can third-party consultation be undertaken. In the
      present case, the CPIO mechanically resorted to third-party consultation
      without any application of mind, and the First Appellate Authority has also
      failed to record any finding that 
Section 8(1)(1)
 applies. Reliance solely on
      third-party refusal is legally impermissible, as a third party has no veto over
      disclosure of information which is otherwise disclosable under the 
RTI Act
.



 
III. MEANING OF "PERSONAL INFORMATION" IS SETTLED LAW
      The Hon'ble Supreme Court in 
CBSE v. Aditya Bandopadhyay
, (2011) 8 SCC
      497, has conclusively held that information which has a direct nexus with a
      public activity or is furnished in discharge of a public duty does not
      constitute "personal information" within the meaning of 
Section 8(1)(j)
 of
      the RTI Act, and that the exemption is confined only to information relating
      purely to the private sphere of an individual. Declarations mandated under
      
Section 44
 of the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013, including disclosures of
      assets, liabilities, and interests, are made solely by virtue of holding a public
      office and for the purpose of ensuring transparency, integrity, and
      accountability in public administration. Such statutory declarations are
      therefore intrinsically linked to public duty and anti-corruption measures,
      and cannot be treated as personal information capable of being withheld
      under 
Section 8(1)(1)
.
 
The interpretation 
laid down in
 
CBSE v. Aditya
      Bandopadhyay
 being law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all
      authorities under 
Article 141
 of the Constitution, and any refusal to disclose
      such information in disregard thereof is ex facie illegal.




      III. VOILATION OF PRINCIPLE OF NAUTRAL JUSTICE
      The impugned order is vitiated by a clear violation of the principles of
      natural justice. The First Appellate Authority failed to afford the Appellant


CIC/MOFIN/A/2024/637801/DGITA                                               Page 4 of 10

       any opportunity of hearing or rebuttal in respect of the submissions made
      by the CPIO. The Appellant was never put to notice nor granted an
      opportunity to appear. Basing an adverse decision on a statute and
      submissions without affording the affected party an opportunity to be
      heard strikes at the root of the doctrine of audi alteram partem and renders
      the impugned order procedurally illegal and unsustainable in law.



      IV. INFORMATION UNDER SECTION 44 OF LOKPAL ACT IS HIGH PUBLIC
      INTEREST

      1. Section 44 mandates public servants to disclose assets and liabilities,
      ensuring transparency and accountability.

      Fighting corruption is the most sacrosanct form of public interest.
      Information under Section 44:

      i.is made by virtue of public office, not private life,
      ii. can not be treated as "personal information" under Section $(1)(j).
      Third-party objection cannot override statutory anti-corruption disclosures.



      The information sought squarely falls within the realm of overriding public
      interest. Declarations mandated under 
Section 44
 of the Lokpal and
      Lokayuktas Act, 2013 are statutory disclosures made by public servants
      solely by virtue of holding public office, with the object of ensuring
      transparency, probity, and accountability in public administration. The
      purpose of such disclosures is to prevent and detect corruption, which has
      repeatedly been recognized as one of the highest and most compelling
      forms of public interest. Information aimed at enforcing anti-corruption
      safeguards cannot be relegated to the realm of private or personal
      information, as it directly concerns the integrity of public institutions and
      the manner in which public power is exercised. Consequently, such
      information cannot be treated as "personal information" within the
      meaning of 
Section 8(1)(j)
 of the RTI Act, and even assuming arguendo that
      any privacy element is involved, the larger public interest in fighting
      corruption decisively outweighs any claimed exemption, mandating
      disclosure.



      V. NEW GROUND CAN NOT BE ADDED
      It is settled law that an order must stand or fall on the reasons recorded
      therein and cannot be sustained on post-facto explanations. As held by the
      Supreme Court in Mohinder Singh Gill v. CEC, the validity of an order cannot
      be improved by subsequent letters or submissions. Since the FAA order does
      not record any reliance on the 
DPDP Act
 or reasoning under 
Section 8(1)(j)
,
      the FAA cannot now justify the denial by invoking DPDP or by filing letters,
      and the impugned order is liable to be set aside.

       VI. DIGITAL PERSONAL DATA PROTECTION ACT, 2023 
DOES NOT APPLY
      The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023
 has no retrospective
      operation. The RTI application in the present case was filed in 2024,
      whereas the 
DPDP Act
 came into force only on 13.11.2025, and the statute
      contains no express or implied provision conferring retrospective effect. It is
      settled law that a statute affecting substantive rights operates
      prospectively unless the legislature clearly provides otherwise, and rights
      which had already accrued under the 
RTI Act
 prior to 13.11.2025 cannot be
      curtailed or taken away by subsequent legislation. Accordingly, the DPDP
      Act is wholly inapplicable to the present case."

Decision:



8.     The Commission after adverting to the facts and circumstances of the
case, hearing both the parties and perusal of the records, observes that the
information sought by the Appellant pertains to detailed asset and liability
declarations of a public servant, namely Shri Ankit Mandraila, Inspector,
Income Tax Department (Group C employee) filed under the CCS (Conduct)
Rules and the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act. Such declarations contain personal
financial details including movable and immovable properties and liabilities,
which are intrinsically personal in nature. The disclosure of these details to a
third-party would amount to an unwarranted invasion of the privacy of the
concerned individual and therefore attract the exemption under 
Section 8(1)(j)

of the RTI Act. The Commission finds that the Appellant has not been able to
demonstrate any specific or overriding larger public interest warranting
disclosure of the said information.



9.     The Commission further notes that the Respondent had undertaken
third-party consultation and the concerned employee expressly declined


CIC/MOFIN/A/2024/637801/DGITA                                          Page 8 of 10

 consent for disclosure of the information. While third-party objection alone
cannot be the sole ground for denial, an independent assessment of the nature
of the information confirms that it squarely falls within the category of
personal information. The observations of the Hon'ble Supreme Court in the
case of 
Girish Ramchandra Deshpande vs. Central Information Commission &
Ors.
 are squarely applicable, wherein it has been held that details relating to
assets, liabilities and service records of an employee constitute personal
information and are exempt from disclosure unless a demonstrable larger
public interest is established.




10. The Commission also takes note of the FAA's observation that the
Appellant and the concerned employee are involved in matrimonial litigation.

In such circumstances, the 
RTI Act
 cannot be permitted to be used as a
mechanism to obtain personal information for pursuing private disputes. The
mere assertion that the information is sought in the interest of transparency or
to fight corruption, without any cogent material substantiating such claim,
cannot outweigh the privacy rights of the individual protected under 
Section
8(1)(j)
 of the Act.



11. In view of the foregoing, the Commission finds no infirmity in the reply
furnished by the CPIO and the order passed by the FAA. However, the
Appellant is at liberty to file and pursue a petition for disproportionate assets
against the third-party.



             The appeal is dismissed accordingly.
